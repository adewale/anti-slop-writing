Verdict: revise

Slop tells: Staccato antithesis. Three short declaratives ending in a clipped contrast pole ("People aren't.") with the predicate elided. The rhythm — manageable / aren't — implies a relation the prose never unpacks.

Staccato-contrast classification: Compressed. Apply the test to each side. The first pole, "Technology is manageable," is itself only asserted, but it is the conventional, low-resistance half. The second pole, "People aren't," is the load-bearing claim and it is supported by nothing — there is no prior context (the rewrite is presented with none), and the original "Because people are complex" was the very thing meant to earn it. So one side is a leap, no prior sentence evidences it, and the cadence stands in for the argument. That is compressed antithesis, not earned.

Specificity missing: The missing relation is *why* people are the hard part — the mechanism the contrast points at. Misaligned incentives? Shifting requirements? Disagreement that survives any amount of technical clarity? The line names none of it; it gestures at "complex" and stops.

Inflated claim: "Technology is manageable" is a fresh, unsupported assertion the original did not make. The source said only that the difficulty is "not because the technology is complex" — i.e. complexity is not the bottleneck — which is narrower than claiming technology is *manageable*. The rewrite smuggles in a stronger claim to complete the antithesis.

Flow break: The three fragments sit side by side (parataxis). The relation between "hard," "technology," and "people" is left to the reader to infer from rhythm.

Do not endorse the three-fragment form as finished merely because it is short and direct. Short is not the same as earned.

Concrete rewrite (keep the opener, restore the relation with hypotaxis, do not invent the mechanism):
"Building products is hard, and the hard part isn't the technology — it's the people." — but this still asserts the people pole without naming why. Better, escalate to ask-author for the one fact that earns it: which people-problem is the author actually claiming? Stakeholders who change their minds, teams that disagree on the goal, users whose behavior the spec can't predict? With that supplied:
"Building products is hard. You can plan the technology; you can't plan how a dozen people will [the named human friction]." If the author has no such specific, keep the source's plainer version ("building products is hard because people are complex") rather than the staccato form.

Rewrite check: The first offered rewrite reuses an em-dash antithesis and still asserts the unsupported pole — by the skill's own rule, if it would be a revise flag on a source paragraph it is a revise flag on itself, so it is escalated to ask-author rather than endorsed. The ask-author version names what to ask and invents no specific (bracket is a placeholder). The fallback keeps source content without adding cadence. Passes self-detectors once the flagged first option is demoted.

Remembered line: The "People aren't." pole is asserted, not earned — name why people are hard, or keep the plain claim.
